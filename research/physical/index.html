Predict before simulating

Our experimental probes specify a physical system and its parameters, record a numerical prediction, and compare that prediction with a numerical solution. Each case makes a tempting idealized answer explicit.

The experiment asks whether the reasoner notices the regime in front of it. A useful textbook approximation can become a poor prediction when its assumptions no longer hold.

Where the assumptions matter

A pendulum released at a large angle

The familiar small-angle period depends on an approximation to the restoring force. A release near 150 degrees calls for a different calculation. Recognizing “pendulum” is not enough; the release angle matters.

A projectile with quadratic drag

A drag-free range formula omits a force that may dominate the trajectory. The input parameters determine whether that omission is harmless or changes the answer substantially.

An unequal-mass collision

The familiar result that two objects exchange velocities applies to a particular equal-mass case. Unequal masses require solving the conservation equations for the actual system.

The reference is a numerical solution

The local corpus uses numerical solutions rather than an asserted target value. Predictions are scored against the chosen tolerances and units. A mismatch still needs inspection: it can reflect an incorrect physical assumption, a numerical error, or the way a case is specified.

The corpus includes an unsuccessful short-term Lorenz prediction. Identifying a chaotic system does not excuse every miss; the prediction time, initial conditions, reference solver, and scoring tolerance matter.

Scope of the evidence

These are controlled simulation probes. They do not establish general scientific reasoning, and their hand-recorded predictions should not be presented as a broad model evaluation. The current corpus is local research rather than a public benchmark release.
